Advertising Sentiment and the Business Cycle: Evidence from The Economist Advertisements¶

Jake Faulkner, Daniel Monteiro and Kiran Perla
SOSC 314 Computational Social Science, Duke Kunshan University (Prof. Markus Neumann), October 2026

GitHub repository: github.com/jakefaulkner316-cell/Jake_Daniel_Kiran_SOSC314 · source notebook: final_report/final_report.ipynb

Summary. Do ads in The Economist sound less positive during U.S. recessions? A raw comparison said yes: economic positive words were 6% below their usual level in recession quarters. The pattern did not survive better measurement. Advertising language drifts over the decades, and the drift is 4 to 40 times larger than the recession gap. Compared fairly over time, the gap shrinks to 1 to 2% and is not statistically reliable. A tighter ad filter that removes 7% of the corpus changes no conclusion. The lesson is about measurement: choices about time, text cleaning and dictionaries move the estimates more than recessions do.

1. Research question and motivation¶

Question. Does the sentiment of advertising language in The Economist differ between U.S. recessions and expansions?

Why it matters. Research on advertising in downturns mostly studies how much firms spend. We study the words they use. If tone tracked the cycle, 67 years of digitised ads would give a long, quarterly record of how business talks in good and bad times. It also tests whether dictionaries built for other texts can measure ad language across seven decades.

Scope. The Economist reaches an affluent, international business readership. Its top advertisers include IBM (1,082 ads) and Barclays (1,029). Our claims concern advertising aimed at this audience only.

Success means a measure that captures advertising language, not other text on the page, and a recession estimate that holds up under fair comparisons over time. A well-tested null counts.

2. Data¶

Source. The Economist Historical Advertisements Master Dataset (Kluge, Gehrmann and Stahl 2023), University of Mannheim: 512,599 ads from all 8,840 issues, 1843 to 2014. Each record has the issue date, a brand name and the full text, read from page scans by Google Vision OCR.

Sample and unit. We keep branded ads from 1948 onward: 172,219 ads in 268 quarters (1948Q1 to 2014Q4), about 640 per quarter. The unit is the single ad; time-series models average ads within each quarter.

Recessions. NBER business-cycle dates. A quarter is a recession if at least two of its three months are in a contraction: 42 quarters in 11 episodes. Counting any recession month gives 48, used as a check. A GDP rule would miss the 1960 to 1961 and 2001 recessions.

Sentiment. Text is lowercased, stripped of non-letters and split into words. A score is the share of words in a dictionary list. The Harvard General Inquirer (Stone et al. 1966) lists general emotional words. Loughran-McDonald (LM; Loughran and McDonald 2011) was built for company filings, so its words are economic. Positive and negative lists give four outcomes. Other preprocessing choices shrink the denominator and inflate shares 11 to 28 times, so every effect is reported as a percentage of the series' own mean.

Ethics. The ads hold no data about readers. The risks are bias and transparency: the corpus shows what large firms told an affluent, mostly Western readership, and an AI model did part of our labelling, which we disclose and check against human labels.

3. Problem setup¶

Task ML type Target Inputs
A. Recession difference in tone Regression (descriptive) Share of words in each dictionary list, per quarter or per ad NBER recession indicator, time controls, advertiser indicators
B. Ad vs non-ad filter Binary classification Is the record a paid message from an outside organisation? Brand field, record length, phrases in the OCR text
C. What ads talk about Topic model, word contrasts Topic shares, word frequencies Ad text, recession, decade

Task B serves Task A. A dictionary score measures advertising language only if the record is an ad, and the corpus also holds bond announcements, legal notices, company accounts and The Economist's own promotions.

Assumptions. Word shares track tone on average, despite ignoring negation and context (Grimmer and Stewart 2013). U.S. NBER dates proxy the climate an international readership faces, since post-war cycles move together (Kose, Sugawara and Terrones 2020). All estimates are descriptive associations, never causal.

4. Methods¶

Part Baseline Improved
Ad filter v1: Week 4 brand blocklist plus length and multi-page flags (removes 1.9%) v2: blocklist matched case-insensitively (2.2%). v3: v2 plus text rules for notices and house content (7.2%)
Inference Raw recession vs expansion comparison of quarterly means Trend or decade effects with HAC errors; ad-level models with decade and advertiser fixed effects
Labels 200 hand labels, 2012 to 2014 only Plus 150 records stratified by decade, coded with a written guide

Filter. The v3 rules are regular expressions in five groups: bond tombstones ("appears as a matter of record only"), legal notices ("notice is hereby given"), financial statements, Economist house content (conferences, subscriptions) and editorial pages. We chose rules over a learned classifier because 24 non-ads are too few to train one, and each removal can be traced to a phrase.

Inference. A five-step ladder: (1) raw quarterly comparison; (2) plus a linear trend; (3) plus decade effects; (4) ad level with decade effects; (5) plus advertiser fixed effects, comparing each firm only with itself. Quarterly models use Newey-West (HAC) errors with 4 lags (Newey and West 1987); ad-level models cluster by year (Cameron and Miller 2015). We ran every combination of 4 outcomes, 2 aggregations, 2 recession definitions and 4 filters: 224 estimates, all reported, so none could be cherry-picked.

Topics and words. A structural topic model (STM; Roberts, Stewart and Tingley 2019) with 10 topics, fitted to 20,000 sampled ads with recession and decade as covariates. Each topic's quarterly share was regressed on recession with decade effects and HAC errors, Holm-corrected for 10 tests (Holm 1979). LDA (Blei, Ng and Jordan 2003) repeated the test. Fightin' words (Monroe, Colaresi and Quinn 2008) compared recession and expansion vocabulary within decades, against a threshold from 500 label shuffles.

5. Evaluation¶

Split. We wrote the filter rules while reading a development set of 150 decade-stratified records, so scores there are optimistic. The held-out test set is Kiran's 200 hand labels (163 ads, 37 non-ads), never used to write the rules. It covers only 2012 to 2014, so a precision audit across all decades complements it. The regressions describe rather than predict, so they rely on HAC errors, time controls and full reporting instead of a split.

Metrics. Recall, the share of non-ads caught, is the filter's goal. Real ads removed matters because dropping genuine ads could bias the corpus. Cohen's kappa (Cohen 1960) corrects agreement for chance, which matters when only 1 record in 5 is a non-ad. Regression effects are a percentage of the series mean, with 95% intervals.

Development labels. An AI model (Claude) labelled all 150 with the guide; Daniel coded 30 blind. First-pass agreement on ad vs non-ad was weak (kappa 0.16), mostly over ads written as prose. We adjudicated the disagreements, starting from AI-proposed resolutions, and added two guide rules. This was not a fully independent reliability test.

5.1 The initial pattern, and why it weakened¶

Figure 1. Quarterly share of positive words, with NBER recession quarters shaded and decade averages in blue.

Raw recession quarters use 6.0% fewer LM positive words and 1.3% fewer Harvard ones. But both series drift far more: down to a low around 1970, then up for four decades. Harvard's decade averages span 1.15 points, 40 times its recession gap; by the standard deviation of decade averages, the drift is 13 times the gap for Harvard and 4 times for LM. Recessions also sit unevenly along it: nine in the 1970s, none after 2009. Quarters are strongly correlated (Durbin-Watson about 0.19; Durbin and Watson 1950), so ordinary errors are too small. Under HAC errors even the raw gaps are not significant (LM positive p = .39).

Notices are 10.7% of 1970s records, the decade where sentiment bottoms out.

Figure 3 and Table 2. Recession effect along the ladder, as a percentage of each series' mean (95% intervals). Orange: no filter. Blue: cleanest filter. Steps 1 to 3 are quarterly; 4 and 5 are ad-level.

Time controls do most of the work: the LM positive gap falls from 6% to 1 to 2%, and no quarterly estimate is significant. Removing 7% of the corpus moves most estimates by under half a point and changes no conclusion; Harvard negative moves most, by 1.3 points, still far from significance. Of all 224 estimates, 6 have p < .05, fewer than the 11 expected by chance.

One estimate stands out. Within advertisers, LM positive is 3.3% lower in recessions (p = .022 with v3): the same firms use fewer positive economic words. It is one significant result among four outcomes, and Harvard shows nothing similar, so it is a lead, not a finding. Composition does not explain it: the sector mix accounts for about 5% of the LM positive gap within decades, and ad and advertiser counts do not change in recessions.

5.4 What advertisers talk about¶

Figure 4. STM topic shares in recession quarters (decade effects, HAC errors). Topic names are our reading of the top words.

Technology and information falls 2.3 points in recessions and industrial equipment rises 1.7, matching the advertiser mix: technology's share of ads falls, energy and industry gain. The evidence is thin. No LDA topic survives the same correction, Philips is the industrial topic's top word, and we have not yet read the top ads per topic.

Table 3. Non-ads that v3 misses on the held-out set, by Kiran's reason.

Most are The Economist's own promotions filed under an outside firm's name, such as Huawei. No phrase rule can fix a wrong brand field. Three are broken OCR, which needs an OCR quality score. Errors also cut the other way: a Deutsche Bank ad was removed because its small print says it "appears as a matter of record only". On the development set, the misses are bank "cards", a name and branch list with no sales message, which phrases cannot separate from real bank ads.

The dictionary reads genre. LM negative was the steadiest Week 5 signal (+5.0%, p = .057 under v2). Under v3 it falls to +3.8% (p = .135). LM was built for filings, so it scores "loss" and "liabilities" in bond notices and balance sheets. Part of the "more negative in recessions" signal was notices, not ads.

Words track campaigns. Fightin' words finds no recession word:

Largest observed scores, all on the recession side: carbide (19.8), dresser (18.7), maybach (17.3), aluminium (17.3), alka (17.2), islamic (15.9). Shuffled labels: median largest |z| 23.1, 95th percentile 72.1; 73% of 500 shuffles beat the real top word. The most extreme shuffled word was philips. Words passing the threshold: 0.

These are brand names: Union Carbide, Dresser Industries, Maybach. One advertiser repeats its words across a quarter, so naive z-scores are overconfident. Shuffled labels beat the real top score in most runs, often through Philips. Word-level differences reflect which firms ran campaigns when.

Checks that changed nothing: 42 vs 48 recession quarters; averaged vs pooled scores (same verdict in 40 of 40 comparisons); all four filters; a pre vs post 1973 interaction (only 17 earlier recession quarters). One result is fragile: on 1990 to 2014 alone the positive gap flips sign, on about 12 recession quarters.

7. Interpretation and substantive takeaways¶

What to conclude. In this corpus there is no reliable evidence that advertising tone changes in recessions. The raw gap is small, mostly reflects slow change in how ads are written, and disappears into the noise once quarters are compared fairly. A filter that catches two thirds of non-ads changes no conclusion, so the null is more convincing than at Week 5.

What it teaches about measurement. Over seven decades the measure moves for reasons unrelated to the economy: copy style drifts, notices come and go, and a finance dictionary reads finance notices as negative. Each moved our estimates more than recessions did. Long-run studies of ad language need time controls, serially robust errors and a validated ad filter.

What not to conclude. That recessions do not affect advertising in general; this is one elite magazine. That anything is causal. Or that the two leads, within-advertiser LM tone and the technology vs industry shift, are findings. They are hypotheses for new data.

8. Limitations and future work¶

Limitations. Bag-of-words scores ignore context. OCR noise varies by decade. Some brand names in the source are wrong. The test labels come from one coder and cover 2012 to 2014; the development labels are mostly AI-coded, with weak first-pass human agreement. U.S. dates only approximate an international readership's conditions. The topic models disagree, and only 47% of ads have a sector.

Future work.

  1. Train a text classifier on more labels and compare it with the rules on the same test set.
  2. Add an independent second coder.
  3. Recompute decade averages under v3, to test whether the 1970s low was partly notices.
  4. Read each topic's top ads, and drop Philips to retest the industrial topic.
  5. Test the within-advertiser LM lead on another publication.
